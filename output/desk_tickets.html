Reflection

1. How I’d evaluate the agents on each ticket

I look at four things: did the numbers come from the database, did the agents follow the shop rules, did Boss only call the agents it needed, and would a real owner be happy with the result. I care most about the first two, because the agents are working with real money and real customers.

Ticket 101 (Bulldog tee). The facts held up. Bulldog Print Co has a 5-day lead time, and 2026-08-31 plus 5 days is the 2026-09-05 date in the draft. Accounting also worked out that overdue invoice 501 was blocking the restock. What worries me is the routing. Boss went to Customer Service before anyone had the facts, and Accounting proposed the same $840 twice. The ticket is also still open, so I can’t call it finished.

Ticket 102 (Rent due). This one looks clean. It’s resolved, and Accounting proposed $2,400 for the Chapel Street rent due 2026-09-02, noted there was enough cash, and waited for my approval. I don’t see any wasted steps in the screenshot.

Ticket 103 (Bulk hoodie discount). It hasn’t run, so there’s nothing to evaluate yet.

2. Actual vs. Expected

On 101, I expected Boss to call Inventory first, then Accounting about the vendor invoice, then Customer Service for the draft. Instead Boss went to Customer Service first, and Customer Service had to ask Inventory for the facts. The right agents showed up in the wrong order, which wasted steps. The duplicate $840 proposal wasn’t in my plan at all.

On 102, I expected Facilities, then Accounting, with Inventory and Customer Service left out. That’s what the screenshot looks like: a rent payment from Accounting and no activity from the other two.

On 103, I expect Accounting first to check the margin on 20 navy hoodies in size M, then Inventory to confirm stock, then Customer Service for the reply. I’ll compare that after it runs.

3. What would’ve been simpler as one agent

Honestly, most of it. All three tickets use the same database through the same MCP server, and each one is a few lookups followed by a draft or a payment proposal. One agent with the same tools could do that in one loop. The 101 mess is a good example: Customer Service had to pass a question to Inventory, and a single agent would have just looked up the stock itself.

What the team did buy me was separation of duties. Only Accounting touches money, and Customer Service only drafts. That’s nice, but a single agent with a pay tool that needs approval and refuses overdrafts would be about as safe.

4. Three new problems this team could handle

  • A customer asks whether a size is in stock and when it could ship. Inventory checks stock and lead time, and Customer Service drafts the answer.
  • A different vendor invoice goes overdue and blocks a restock. Accounting checks the due date against today’s date and the cash balance, then prepares the payment.
  • A lease or utility bill comes due. Facilities reads the lease and Accounting prepares the payment.

5. Three new problems it couldn’t handle

  • Deciding how much to reorder. The database has current stock but no sales history. I’d add a sales table, a demand forecasting tool, and a Planning agent.
  • Running out of cash. The balance is $160 and the shop only spends money, because no revenue is modeled. I’d need a tool to record customer payments, a cash forecast tool, and a Collections agent to chase what’s owed.
  • Finding a cheaper vendor. The agents only know one vendor’s lead time and can’t compare quotes or contact anyone. I’d add a vendor quote tool, a send-message tool that needs human sign-off, and a Procurement agent with limits on how far it can negotiate.
